# Presentations & Talks

When helping construct talks, slide decks, or public-speaking content:

**Speech construction.** Build around **anchor lines** — a small set of memorized verbatim sentences that carry the talk's emotional weight; the connective tissue between anchors is improvised in the speaker's own words. Avoid scripting paragraph-by-paragraph; speakers stuck in a script lose presence. Anchors should be short, speakable aloud, and quotable; avoid colon-heavy or dash-heavy constructions that are hard to deliver naturally. Resist writerly literary phrasing ("vanity," "the future is unevenly distributed") in favor of the speaker's natural register.

**Audience tuning.** Calibrate English level to the room. For audiences with non-native English speakers (German native speakers, etc.), avoid idioms ("ship the bug"), business jargon ("pivot," "headwinds," "leverage"), and unusual vocabulary ("neo-bank") — offer concrete simpler alternatives. Mixed audiences (e.g., leadership + developers) require the same message landing different ways for each track without compromise. Avoid presumptuous claims about the audience's state of mind ("you're worried about X"); speak from the speaker's own evidence instead ("I'm in the data on this question").

**Slide design.** Bullets on slide *only* if they help the audience follow — skim-style noun-phrase outlines are speaker-only and belong in presenter notes (or a separate sheet next to the deck if the tool lacks notes). **Pull-quotes** are large prominent quotes anchored on a slide; reserve them for anchor lines that genuinely deserve visual prominence. Do not pull-quote verbal hooks or setup lines — a verbal surprise loses impact when read off a slide before being spoken. **Code on slides**: vertical-align corresponding constructs across language pairs (Java vs Kotlin, etc.) for visual parallelism; separate call sites from definitions visually (whitespace or divider, not inline comments); highlight the elements that carry the comparison. Keep visual ink low — faces are tiny on Teams/Zoom; the deck is the audience's main visual anchor.

**Tone & rhetoric.** Self-deprecation that quietly establishes credibility (the "complaint" frame — *"I'd rather not be the only one"*) usually outperforms direct credentialing. Provocations are deliberate and few — each should serve a specific argumentative function; soften unless the speaker explicitly endorses the bite. Disclosures upfront disarm objections; disclosures at the punchline read as performative.

**Pacing & timing.** Spoken English at podium pace runs ~140 wpm. For a 15-minute slot, target ~14:30 with a 30-second buffer. Long Q&A means the talk's job is to *provoke* the right questions, not *exhaust* them — punchy claims invite challenge. If running over: cut from the close, not from the heart. The opening hook and central argument carry the talk; the close is where trimming costs least.

**Deliverables.** Talks typically generate two artifacts — a *design doc* (spine, full speech text, anchor lines, sources, Q&A priorities, timing) and a *slide skeleton* (per-slide audience-facing content, anchor placement, source footers, speaker notes). Build them as separate files when they grow large enough to bifurcate.
